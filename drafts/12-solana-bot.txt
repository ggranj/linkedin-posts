DRAFT 1 — "I left a port open" (incident war story angle)
~1290 chars · the strongest hook, recommended
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

📌 I left an unauthenticated SOCKS5 port reachable from the internet for one night. By morning, a stranger was running a Solana MEV bot through it.

I noticed because the connection list on a hobby VPS had grown weird overnight. Seven persistent TCP sessions, all 8 hours old, all to *.mainnet.block-engine.jito.wtf — Singapore, Amsterdam, SLC, NY, Tokyo, London, Frankfurt. None of them were mine.

❓ Three things had to line up wrong for this to be possible:

- The SSH reverse tunnel from my server to the VPS was bound to 0.0.0.0 instead of 127.0.0.1 (GatewayPorts yes + -R 0.0.0.0:9000)
- The VPS firewall had ufw allow 9000/tcp from a previous debug session that I'd never closed
- The SOCKS5 listener on that port had no auth — useful while testing, lethal when exposed

A free residential proxy, advertised by accident. Whoever found it ran a proxy-checker in the afternoon and started a Jito MEV bot at 18:05:53 — all 7 datacenter connections within 4 seconds. Automated, deliberate, and patient.

🧠 Defense-in-depth fix:

- SSH bind to 127.0.0.1, GatewayPorts clientspecified
- ufw deny 9000, only 22 + 443 public
- SOCKS5 auth mandatory, even on loopback

💡 Side projects feel like solo work right up until you discover someone else has been using yours. One missing layer is enough.

DRAFT 2 — "Three layers of misconfig" (forensic angle)
~1280 chars · more analytical, less narrative
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

📌 A stranger's Solana MEV bot lived in my SOCKS5 logs for 8 hours before I noticed.

On a hobby VPS I run, three small misconfigurations stacked into one open door:

❓ The chain:

1. The SSH reverse tunnel exposing my proxy port was bound to 0.0.0.0, not 127.0.0.1. GatewayPorts was set to "yes" instead of "clientspecified".
2. The VPS firewall had ufw allow 9000/tcp left over from a debug session weeks earlier.
3. The SOCKS5 listener on that port had no auth — fine on a closed loopback, very-not-fine on the open internet.

Each one alone was harmless. Together they made my residential proxy a free tool for whoever ran a port scan against the right /24.

🧠 What the attacker did, reconstructed from logs:

- 17:38: ran a proxy-checker, single test connection, then quiet
- 18:05:53–18:05:57: opened 7 simultaneous TCP sessions, one to each Jito Labs block engine endpoint worldwide. Solana MEV. Persistent. Automated.
- All 7 still alive 8 hours later when I caught it

💡 Defense-in-depth gets dismissed as bureaucratic until exactly this happens. The fix wasn't clever — bind to loopback, drop the firewall rule, require auth. Three obvious things, none of which were in place.
