DRAFT 2 - "The race condition in my own code" (war story angle)
~1280 chars · most concrete, easy to relate to
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

📌 I had a race condition in my own Go service for weeks. The fix was four lines.

Two goroutines were independently initialising the same lazy singleton. Each ended up with its own atomic counters. The dashboard always read zero - handlers wrote to one instance, the API exposed the other.

❓ The clue was sitting in plain sight: two log lines, identical content, one millisecond apart at startup. "loaded from file" appearing twice. Once you see it, you can't unsee it.

🧠 Fix: sync.Once around each singleton, sync.Mutex around the shutdown slice. Took longer to write the commit message than the code.

What this taught me - the kind of thing only personal projects let you learn:

- TOCTOU is sneaky. "Field is nil → make it" is two operations, not one.
- Two near-identical log lines a millisecond apart should be a smell.
- "It only happens sometimes" usually means "two goroutines see the same nil at the same time".

💡 At work, code review would have caught this before merge. On a side project, you ship it, watch it lie to you for weeks, then learn the lesson the hard way. That's the gym I keep coming back to - and, honestly, the same gym I'm betting will eventually pay rent. Times being what they are, you build for craft and for survival at the same time.

DRAFT 3 - "Active vs passive detection" (technical insight angle)
~1300 chars · most "useful" - generalisable lesson
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

📌 "Auto-detect" usually means "trust whatever the kernel reports". On my own service I rewrote it to actually verify.

In a Go service I run on the side, IPv6 routing detection used to walk the network interfaces and check for a global-unicast address. If there was one, "v6 works". Easy. Wrong.

❓ Some residential ISPs hand out a global v6 address via SLAAC and then quietly black-hole every packet you send. The interface check passes; the connection times out three minutes later. "auto-detect" was lying confidently.

🧠 New approach: fire a 28-byte DNS query at three v6 anycast resolvers in parallel. Win on the first valid reply within 1.5s. Give up after 2s. Cache the result. ~120 lines of Go, no extra dependencies.

💡 The pattern generalises. Passive detection (read state, infer reality) is cheap and often wrong. Active detection (poke reality, watch what comes back) is slightly more expensive and tells the truth.

Same shape shows up everywhere - "is the DB alive?" needs more than ping; "is this feature flag on?" needs more than reading cache. Personal projects are where I get to learn these patterns without a JIRA ticket - and where, hopefully, a few of them eventually become something worth charging for. Times being what they are, sharpening the craft and building the side income are the same workout.
